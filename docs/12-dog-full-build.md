# THE DOG AT THE DOOR — Full Snowflake Build (Steps 1–8)

**DarkHearts Ltd.** · Dir. B. L. Barkey · Target runtime **4:12** · 43 shots
**Target prize: Audience Choice — $100,000.**

---

## Step 1 — Concept Sentence

> **A shiba waits at the door of a house being taken apart around him.**

*Character:* a dog. *Engine:* the house emptying on a schedule he isn't told about. *Irony:* every human in the film knows what happened and none of them can say it to him — so the furniture says it instead.

**Controlling idea:** *A creature who cannot be told what happened arrives at the truth anyway, by furniture.*

### Why this film exists in the slate

Audience Choice pays **$100,000** — level with third place — and is decided by popularity rather than by the jury. That is a fundamentally different optimization, and this is the cheapest film on either slate to produce. It is also the **fallback film** named in `08-production-plan.md` §6: if the calendar collapses, this is the one that can still be finished and still be complete.

**It is not a lesser film. It is a narrower one.** Everything below is built to make the narrowness the point.

---

## Step 2 — Beat Paragraph

1. A shiba inu lies in a hallway facing a front door, in a house full of boxes.
2. Over three days — light arcing across the same frame, boxes leaving, furniture leaving, then only the pale rectangles on the wallpaper where pictures hung — the house empties around the dog, who does not move from the spot.
3. Movers carry out a recliner, and the dog stands for the first time in the film, walks to where the chair was, and lies down inside the empty shape of it.
4. A woman crouches in the open doorway with a leash and waits — not calling, not coaxing, just down on the floor at his level — until he comes.
5. The last shot is the empty house and the rectangle in the carpet where the chair stood.

## Step 3 — Want / Need / Lie / Ghost

| | |
|---|---|
| **Want** | For him to come home. |
| **Need** | Nothing. *(Deliberate. See below.)* |
| **Lie** | *"He is late."* |
| **Ghost** | He is not late. |
| **Arc** | **Not a character arc.** The dog does not grow. The dog **relocates**. |
| **Value shift** | **The Door → The Chair.** From waiting for a return to keeping a shape. |

### Who actually arcs

**The audience.** This is the whole design and it must be protected in every production decision.

The dog is a flat protagonist. He learns nothing, decides nothing he could articulate, and is exactly as loyal in the last shot as in the first. What moves is the viewer, along a fixed three-stage path:

| Stage | Shots | What the viewer is doing |
|---|---|---|
| **Curiosity** | 1–16 | *Why is the house being emptied? Where is everyone?* |
| **Recognition** | 17–28 | *Oh. Someone died. It was the person in that chair.* |
| **Grief** | 29–43 | *…and the dog knows.* |

The third stage is the film. The audience getting there is worth nothing on its own — audiences reach "someone died" inside ninety seconds and feel briefly clever about it. **The devastation is the dog getting there**, and it lands on exactly one shot (31), where he chooses the chair's shape over the door.

> **The rule that follows from this:** the film must never be ahead of the audience *and* never behind them. Stay level. The moment the film signals that it knows what it's doing — a swell, a dissolve, a cutaway to a photograph — the audience stops assembling and starts being told, and a told grief is a greeting card.

### `09-one-pager.md` explicitly exempts this film

> *"A flat protagonist. Some shorts arc the audience instead of the character. Legitimate and powerful — but then stop calling it a Hero's Journey and stop trying to force beat 9."*

**Do not map this film to the nine-beat table.** It has its own structure and it is the one above.

### Supporting figures — deliberately faceless

- **THE MOVERS** — legs, hands, and torsos. Never a face. Never a word that resolves into language.
- **THE WOMAN** — the man's adult daughter, though the film never says so. Seeded in Beat 2 as a pair of jeans and a hand that refills a water bowl. **Hers is the only face in the film**, and she has to come down to the floor to get into the frame at all.
- **THE MAN** — never appears, never named, never photographed. He is a depression in a cushion, a blanket folded over an arm, a jacket on a hook, and a rectangle in the carpet.

*There is no dialogue in this film. Not one intelligible word.*

## Step 5 — The Turn

**Shot 31. 2:53.**

The recliner is out of the house. The living room is bare. In the middle of the carpet there is a clean, unfaded rectangle with four dents in it, and a plaid blanket on the floor where it slid off the arm.

The dog comes in, crosses to the rectangle, turns once, and **lies down inside the shape of the chair.**

That is the shot. He does not go back to the door. For four minutes the front door has been the only thing in his world with a future attached to it, and he gives it up — not because he was called, coaxed, fed, or led, but because he worked it out. The Lie dies there: *he is late* becomes *he is not coming*, in a creature with no language to hold either sentence in.

*What the audience sees change:* **direction.** Every frame of the film until 2:47 has the dog oriented toward the front door. From 2:53 he is oriented toward an empty rectangle on the floor of a room he has never once entered in the film. Nothing else about him changes — same posture, same chin, same ears. Only which way he is facing.

> **Protect this shot above all others.** It is the film. Beats 4 and 5 are its consequence and Beats 1–3 are its setup. If you have one shot's worth of generation budget left on Aug 26, spend it here.

## Step 6 — Look Bible

### The reference
A locked-off hallway and a house being emptied. No cinematography that a person carrying a box would have noticed. The camera is not a witness with opinions — **the camera is furniture that got left behind.**

### Camera — the whole grammar in one line

> **The camera is 18 inches off the floor and it never moves.**

Eighteen inches is a shiba's eye height. That single decision does four jobs at once:

1. **It is the point of view of the film,** without a single POV shot or a single subjective effect.
2. **It crops every human at the chest or lower** — which is why the woman's crouch works (below).
3. **It makes the furniture enormous** and the emptying physical rather than sentimental.
4. **It eliminates almost all human-face generation from the film.** See Step 7.

**Four camera positions. One lens. Zero movement.**

| Pos | Setup | Shots | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A · THE MASTER** | Hallway centreline, 18" high, front door center-rear ~14 ft away. Living-room doorway open frame-left, coat hook frame-right. | 21 | The film's home. Everything happens in or through this frame. |
| **B · THE THRESHOLD** | 18" high, 4 ft from the door, facing it square. Bottom of the door, the sill, the gap of daylight underneath. | 7 | The outside world, admitted only as light and shadow — until the woman crouches into it. |
| **C · THE CHAIR** | 18" high, in the living-room doorway, looking at the recliner. | 11 | The other room. The chair, and later the absence of the chair. |
| **D · THE HOOK** | 18" high, frame-right hall wall. The coat hook, the leash, the jacket. | 4 | The exposition object. Three states: full, thinning, empty. |

No dolly, no push, no pan, no tilt, no handheld, **no drift.** Not once, not subtly, not "just a little life." Four locked geometries for four minutes.

*(This is also the single biggest consistency gift available in AI video. Four locked plates regenerate reliably; a moving camera does not. See Step 7.)*

### Lens
**35mm. One lens. There is no second lens in this film.**

Deep stop — **everything in frame is sharp, always.** No shallow focus anywhere, ever. Shallow depth of field is the most common "prettiness" tell in AI video *and* it implies an operator making choices, which contradicts the entire premise. The camera is furniture. Furniture does not rack focus.

> SILT spends three focal lengths and rations the third to four shots. DOG spends one. That contrast is a studio statement, and both films are defensible for the same reason: *the lens has a job and it never does another one.*

### Light
**One source for the whole film: the glass in the front door.**

Everything you see is that light or the falloff from it. The film's clock is not a watch — it is where the two hard bars of door-glass light are sitting:

| Time of day | Where the light is | Quality |
|---|---|---|
| Morning | Two hard vertical bars on the hall floor, pointing at camera | Hard, high contrast, dust in the air |
| Midday | Short, flat, almost under the door | Flat, colorless |
| Afternoon | Bars climbing the frame-left wall, long and orange | Low, raking, warm |
| Evening | No bars. The glass is a grey rectangle. The dog is a silhouette. | Ambient only, blue |
| Night | One ugly off-frame bulb from a back room | Wrong color, wrong direction — the only "bad" light in the film, and it is bad on purpose |

**One exception, declared:** in Beat 3 the front door is propped open and the source becomes the full open doorway. That is still the same source, just uncovered. There is no third state.

**No practical lamps. No ceiling fixtures. No fill.** If a shot needs a second source, the shot is wrong.

### Palette

> **The dog is the only saturated color in the film.**

A shiba's red-and-cream coat, against cream walls, honey floorboards graded flat, brown-grey cardboard, and pale carpet. Everything that isn't the dog sits between 20% and 40% saturation. The pale rectangles on the wallpaper — where pictures hung and the paper never faded — are the film's second-brightest thing and they are just *cleaner beige*.

**Then the ending states itself in color:** in the last two shots the dog is gone, and there is no saturated color left anywhere in the frame. Nobody will consciously notice. Everybody will feel the house go dead.

*(This is the exact rhyme of SILT's rule — "hi-vis orange is only ever Eli." One studio, one grammar.)*

### Grain & texture
16mm-weight grain in every frame — the DarkHearts signature and the cheapest anti-tell available. Dust visible in the light bars on every morning shot. Scuffed baseboards, a worn patch on the floorboards in front of the door, dents in the carpet. **Nothing new, nothing clean, nothing staged.**

### The three rules you never break

1. **The camera is at 18 inches and it does not move.**
2. **One light source: the door. You can always point at it.**
3. **No human face above the knee — except once.**

## Step 7 — Consistency Sheet

DOG's consistency problem is structurally easier than SILT's and it is worth understanding exactly why, because it is the reason this film is the fallback: **43 shots, but only four camera geometries.** Every shot is a relight or a redress of one of four locked plates. Generate the four plates first, freeze them, and the rest of the film is variations on assets you have already approved.

### The face discipline — the highest-leverage decision in the film

> **There is exactly one human face in this film. It appears in three shots.**

Because the camera is at 18 inches and never moves, every human is cropped at the chest or below. Movers are legs and hands. The woman is jeans and sneakers for her first three appearances. **Human face drift — the failure mode that kills more entries than any other — is simply not present in 40 of 43 shots.**

Then in Beat 4 she crouches down onto the sill, into the dog's eyeline, and her face enters the frame for the first time in the film. In shot 40 she stands up and it leaves again, and only her legs remain.

**That is a devastating emotional move executed entirely with camera height.** No coverage, no close-up, no push-in. She becomes visible by getting down to his level and stops being visible by standing up. Papamichael will read it as blocking. Catmull will read it as the whole point. It costs three generations.

### The leash — the mentor object

`09-one-pager.md`, rule 2: *"The mentor is an object. A letter, a map, a survey plat, a leash, a photograph."*

**It is the leash.** It hangs on the hook from the first minute and nobody touches it for three and a half. It carries every piece of exposition a person would otherwise have to speak: a leash means walks, walks mean a person, a leash still on its hook means the walks have stopped, and a leash moving from the hook into a woman's hand is custody changing owners. Generate it **once**, lock the asset, and reuse it across the Position D shots.

Its three states are the film's spine in miniature:

| Shot | State | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| **2** | Leash + a man's canvas work jacket | Someone lives here and walks this dog |
| **17** | Leash alone; the jacket is gone | The person is being packed away |
| **42** | Bare hook | It's over, and the dog is somebody's now |

### Exposure discipline — the dog

The dog appears in **29 of the 43 shots** and he must hold across all of them. *(The other 14 are the empty room, the hook, and the threshold — geometry with no character in it, and therefore free.)* Three mitigations, in order of importance:

1. **Posture, never emotion.** *Never write "sad," "lonely," "hopeful," "longing," or "waiting" into a prompt.* Write the body: *"lying with chin flat on the floorboards, forelegs extended, ears back, eyes open, facing the door."* The model cannot render an emotion; it will render a **performance** of one, and a performing dog is the exact failure this film cannot survive. **Emotion is the audience's job. Give them a posture and get out of the way.**
2. **He is in the same posture in most of the film.** Chin down, facing the door, from behind or three-quarter-rear. That is a small, repeatable target.
3. **He is frequently a silhouette or partially in falloff** — which is what a dog lying between a camera and a bright door actually looks like.

## Score & Sound

Per `07-score-and-sound.md` §6 — *"the inverse of SILT. Near-total silence, no score until 3:00, then one sustained E2 drone under the last minute and nothing else. Same root note, opposite deployment."*

| Cue | Time | Content |
|---|---|---|
| **— no score —** | 0:00–3:12 | **Three minutes and twelve seconds with no music of any kind.** Only the house: fridge, clock, tape gun, furniture on boards, rain, traffic, and voices that never resolve into words. |
| **M1 · "E2"** | 3:12–4:12 | One sustained **82.4 Hz** drone. It enters under Shot 34 at −45 dB and rises 8 dB across the last minute. **No melody. No second note. No resolution. No swell.** It stops on the cut to black — **cut, not faded.** |

### The refrigerator is the film's drone until it isn't

The house hums at roughly 120 Hz from the first frame. At **1:43 (Shot 19) the power is cut and the hum stops** — and the film drops into a genuine, uncomfortable silence for ninety seconds. Then at 3:12 the E2 arrives and fills the hole the fridge left.

**The house's own hum is replaced by the studio's root note.** Nobody will be able to tell you why the last minute feels different. It is because the room started breathing again on the film's terms.

### The sound reservations

Three sounds are held back and spent exactly once or twice. This is DOG's equivalent of SILT's four 85mm shots.

| Sound | Spent | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Nails on floorboards** | Shots 29 and 39 only | The dog is lying down for 38 shots. The first time you hear him move, it means everything. |
| **True silence** | 1:43–3:12 | Bought by having an audible fridge for the first 103 seconds. |
| **The E2** | 3:12 | Bought by three minutes of not having it. |

### The forbidden list

No piano. No strings. No solo cello. **No solo cello.** No music box, no ticking-clock rhythm section, no ambient pad, no vocal texture, no rising tone under the woman's crouch, no sting on the chair leaving, and **no sound cue whatsoever on Shot 31.** The turn plays in silence or it does not play.

## Production Notes

### The four expensive shots

**26, 31, 36, 40.** These get 3× the generation budget of anything else, and they are generated **first**, in look dev, before any of the easy hallway holds.

- **26** — the chair passing the dog, and the head turn. Spine of the hook clip.
- **31** — the turn. The film.
- **36** — the only face in the film.
- **40** — the clip-on and the stand, where the face leaves the frame. Pure geometry, and it has to be exact.

### Four plates, not forty-three setups

This is why DOG is the cheap film and the fallback film. Generate and freeze **four plates** — A, B, C, D — then treat all 43 shots as relights and redresses of those four. Concretely:

1. Build the four positions as stills first (Cinema Studio Image 2.5 for the empty geometry, Soul Cinema for anything with the dog in it).
2. Approve them. **Then never regenerate the geometry again** — only the light state and the set dressing.
3. Animate only what needs to move. In **more than half the film — roughly 21 of the 43 shots — nothing moves at all** except dust in a light bar and the dog breathing. Those are the cheapest clips you will ever generate, and they are the *point* of the film rather than a compromise.

> **Two-stage beats one-stage by a wide margin at these keep rates** (`10-tiers-and-pipeline.md` §4). DOG is the film where that advantage is largest, because the still *is* the shot.

### Model pipeline

Following `10-tiers-and-pipeline.md` §4, with one open question:

| Stage | Model | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Identity lock | **Soul Cast** | Run it on the dog. ⚠️ **Soul Cast is described as "consistent cinematic character *identity*" and may be tuned for humans.** Test this on Aug 12 before committing — it is the one unverified assumption in this build. |
| **Fallback if Soul Cast won't hold an animal** | **Seedance 2.0** with `image_references` | Generate one hero still of the dog, approve it, and feed it as a reference into every subsequent shot. This is the more robust path for a non-human subject and may simply be the right answer. |
| Plates | **Cinema Studio Image 2.5** | 21:9, 4K. The four empty geometries. |
| Keyframes | **Soul Cinema** | Accepts `soul_id`. Everything with the dog. |
| Motion | **Cinema Studio Video 3.0** | `genre: drama`, `generate_audio: false`. Use `start_image` + `end_image` for the light-arc shots — you have both ends already. |

**Audio generation off everywhere.** `generate_audio: false`, `sound: "off"`. This film is 75% silence by design and you do not want a model's guess at what an empty house sounds like. It also costs fewer credits.

### Aspect ratio — and the one place this film differs from SILT

Shoot **21:9**, same as SILT, for the same reason: an ASC juror reads an anamorphic frame as intent.

**But DOG is the Audience Choice film, and Audience Choice is decided on phones.** A 21:9 frame on a vertical feed is a letterbox slot about an inch tall. That is a real conflict and it has to be resolved *before* generation, not after:

> **Compose every shot so the subject sits dead center, because the vertical crop takes the middle third.**
>
> Door center-rear in A. Her face center in B. The rectangle center in C. The hook center in D.

Then use **`reframe`** (`10-tiers-and-pipeline.md`) to cut a genuine 9:16 version of the hook clip rather than pillarboxing the 21:9. The film stays 21:9 for the jury; the campaign asset is natively vertical. This costs nothing if you compose for it from Shot 1 and is unfixable if you don't.

### The hook clip — "The Chair" (15s)

`08-production-plan.md` §4 designates this and it is **built, not harvested**: Shots 26 → 29 → 31, trimmed to 15 seconds.

The chair goes past him. He stands. He lies down in its shape.

No text, no context, no sound needed, no language. It is legible to anyone on earth in fifteen seconds and it is the single most shareable asset in this entire package. **Cut it in 9:16 first and 21:9 second** — the vertical is the one that travels.

### Rules compliance — one risk that is specific to this film

SILT's compliance risk is the religious clause. **DOG's is trademarks**, and it is easy to miss: this film is set inside a house being packed into boxes and loaded onto a truck. That is an environment absolutely full of brand-legible objects.

- **Blank cardboard.** No printed boxes, no branded tape.
- **No logo on the moving truck.** Plain white panel.
- **No visible product design** — no recognizable appliance badges, no TV, no soda can, no cereal box, no phone with a legible logo.
- **No printed matter** anywhere that a viewer could read.

Every prompt in this film should carry a negative clause on visible branding. Add it to the locked location fragments before the first generation, not after the first frame with a logo in it.

*(Political statements: none. Religious statements: none. This film is clean on both. It is also entirely non-verbal, which means no translation, no captions, and no language-based ambiguity anywhere in it.)*

## What Is Deliberately Absent

The risk register calls sentimentality **the whole risk of this film**. It is not managed by taste during the edit — it is managed by a list of things you decide, now, not to shoot. Every one of these is a real temptation and every one of them turns this film into a greeting card.

1. **No photograph of the man.** Not on a wall, not in a box, not in her hands, not in the last shot. **Ever.** The chair is the photograph.
2. **No dissolves.** Hard cuts only, all 43 of them. A dissolve is the film sighing.
3. **No score before 3:12.** Not a pad, not a tone, not "just a little something under it."
4. **No sad-dog performance.** Never prompt an emotion. The dog is neutral and the *context* is devastating. A dog performing grief is a commercial for pet insurance.
5. **No slow motion.** Anywhere. For any reason.
6. **No crying.** She doesn't. Nobody does.
7. **No other creature.** No second dog, no cat, no child in the doorway.
8. **No rain at the end, no sunset, no golden hour.** The last day is grey, overcast, and completely ordinary. *The day someone comes for the dog is a Tuesday.*
9. **The dog never eats or drinks on camera.** Eating reads as "he's fine." Refusing food reads as neglect and turns the film into an ASPCA ad. The bowl is set down full in Shot 16 and it is still full in Shot 18 and the film never mentions it again.
10. **Nobody speaks an intelligible word.** Voices exist — muffled, in other rooms, never resolving. Not one line of dialogue, not one call of his name.
11. **No end card text.**

### On that last one

SILT earns a three-line card because SILT is a film with a historical fact underneath it. DOG has no fact to state, and **anything the card could say, the film has already said better.** Hold black for two seconds after Shot 43, then the DarkHearts mark and the required Higgsfield watermark. Nothing else.

*The one alternate worth considering — and only one — is a card carrying the dog's name, which no one in the film ever says aloud. It is a real gut-punch and it is also exactly one degree too clever. **Recommendation: no card.** Shoot it, cut both, watch both on a phone, and trust whichever one you don't want to explain.*

| Risk | Sev | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| **Sentimentality** — the film becomes a greeting card | **High** | The eleven prohibitions above, decided in pre-production. This is not an edit-room judgment call. |
