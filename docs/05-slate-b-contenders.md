# Slate B — The Contenders

Four concepts engineered against the actual rubric and the actual jury. Each is scored against the five weighted criteria, with the reasoning shown so you can argue with it.

**Rubric reminder:** Cinematic Quality 25 · Storytelling & Creativity 25 · Technical Execution 20 · Platform Engagement 15 · Social Media Engagement 15.

---

# B1 · SILT
### *The flagship. Recommended greenlight.*

**Step 1 — Concept Sentence**
> A dam engineer finds the drowned town below still keeping time — and learns the dam has been ringing its bell for sixty-five years.

**The idea in one move.** Take A1's emotional truth — the engineer who maintains the structure that erased his family — and give it the missing engine: **the dam's turbines share a harmonic with the drowned church bell.** For sixty-five years the machine has been striking that bell underwater, once a day, and nobody has ever heard it because there was always water on top of it. This drought is the first time in a human lifetime the sound has had anywhere to go.

That is not a metaphor bolted onto an engineering story. **Forced harmonic excitation of a structure is real physics**, and you are the one person in this contest who can shoot it with the right vocabulary.

**Step 2 — Beat Paragraph**
1. Eli Vance, P.E., walks a Colorado reservoir drained to a sixty-five-year low, running a drawdown inspection against an 18:00 refill order.
2. Below the old waterline he finds the street grid of Redstone and, in it, his grandfather's front steps — four concrete treads and a chimney, and nothing else.
3. Sitting there past his own schedule, he hears a bell, follows it, and finds that the "hazard marker" he has logged in every report of his career is the top of a forty-foot church tower — and the bell inside it is humming at the exact turbine frequency printed on his own inspection sheet.
4. The radio confirms the gates are opening while he is standing in the tower.
5. At 18:00 he swings the bell himself — one beat *ahead* of the machine — and then walks out and lets the water come.

**Step 3 — Want / Need / Lie / Ghost**
- **Want:** finish the inspection and sign the report.
- **Need:** to stop serving the thing that took from him, and to answer it instead.
- **Lie:** *"If I maintain it perfectly, it wasn't a crime."*
- **Ghost:** in 1961 his grandfather Tom Vance was carried out of Redstone by deputies and died within the year; Eli grew up to become the engineer who keeps the dam running.
- **The Turn (Step 5, previewed):** Shot 44. He has the radio in his hand and the authority to call a delay. He sets it down on the sill and climbs instead. **He stops trying to stop it, and starts trying to be heard over it.**

**Step 4 — Runtime Map (4:22)**

| Beat | Time | Shots | Content |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cold open | 0:00–0:22 | 3 | Black. Underwater. One bell strike. Title. |
| 1 | 0:22–1:05 | 10 | Drought. Truck. Boat ramp ending in dirt. The 18:00 clock. |
| 2 | 1:05–2:00 | 13 | The walk down. The grid. The mailbox. The steps. He sits. |
| 3 | 2:00–3:10 | 16 | The sound. The tower. The climb. The frequency match. |
| 4 | 3:10–3:40 | 8 | Radio: gates opening. He sets it down. |
| 5 | 3:40–4:22 | 11 | 18:00. He rings it. The water. The tower goes under. Card. |
| | **Total** | **61 shots** | avg 4.3s |

### Why It Wins

**Cinematic Quality (25%) — the strongest card in the deck.** A cracked silt plain under hard drought light, a single figure in hi-vis orange as the only saturated thing in frame, and a headlamp inside a submerged stone tower. This is *Nebraska* in a reservoir. Papamichael's entire body of work is available light and motivated sources, and this film has exactly two lighting setups: **the sun, and one headlamp.** You can defend every frame.

**Storytelling & Creativity (25%).** A complete want→need arc with a nameable turn at a nameable shot, wrapped in a premise nobody else will have. The "machine has been ringing the drowned town's bell this whole time" reveal is genuinely novel and it is *earned by the physics*, not asserted.

**Technical Execution (20%).** Structurally, this is a film about **one man, one costume, mostly wide and mid, frequently backlit or lamp-lit.** That is not an aesthetic accident — it is the correct answer to AI video's hardest problem. Character drift kills entrants; SILT limits clean frontal close-ups to a handful of deliberate moments and hides the rest behind hardhat brim, dust, and backlight. The refill set piece is water and scale, which is precisely what these models are *best* at.

**Platform Engagement (15%).** The open-source package practically writes itself: real HEC-RAS terminology, a real drawdown procedure, an annotated frequency-match, prompt cards showing how the tower was held consistent across sixteen shots. Genuinely educational, genuinely browsable.

**Social Media Engagement (15%).** The hook clip is *pre-built into the story*: **the water arriving.** Twelve seconds, works silent, works on a phone, needs no context. And the headline is free — *"A licensed dam engineer made a film about a drowned town. The physics in it is real."*

**Producibility:** ★★★★☆ — 61 shots, one character, two lighting states, one big VFX beat.
**Risk:** the tower interior needs to hold across ~16 shots. Mitigate with a locked reference and the shot design in `06-silt-full-build.md` §7.

**Score projection: strongest overall. → Full build in `docs/06-silt-full-build.md`, screenplay in `screenplay/SILT.md`.**

# B2 · ROLL
### *The Anderson play. Second priority.*

**Step 1 — Concept Sentence**
> Every time he taps, the roll restarts — and the opponent has been his own shoulder all along.

*(Full Steps 2–4 in `04-slate-a-personal.md` § A4.)*

### Why It's Here

**The loop is a technical thesis, not just a story device.** AI video's characteristic failure is that repeated shots drift. ROLL is *built* out of repeated shots, and the drift reads as escalating wrongness — the film's own unease. You are converting the medium's defect into its subject. That is the kind of move Catmull the technologist notices, and it is a *great* open-source writeup: *"I made the model's weakness the film's style, and here's the shot data."*

**Anderson gets a fight.** Six of them. Escalating, kinetic, no dialogue.
**Catmull gets a real turn** — the man wins by surrendering.
**Papamichael gets** one hard practical over a mat in a dark gym: a single source, hot top-light, deep falloff, bodies half in shadow. Extremely defensible, extremely cheap.

**Producibility:** ★★★★★ — one location, one light, two bodies, ~54 shots, no exteriors, no crowds, no water.
**Risk:** two-body physical interaction is where AI video most visibly breaks (limb merging, impossible grips). Mitigate by shooting tight and partial — hands, a foot, a face against the mat, a shoulder — rather than clean full-body wides of the grapple. Which is *also* how real grappling is shot.

**Score projection:** highest Technical Execution ceiling of the four; slightly lower Cinematic Quality (one interior can't compete with a drained valley). **Best second entry.** Different lane, different look, different audience from SILT — no cannibalization.

# B3 · THE DOG AT THE DOOR
### *The Audience Choice play. Run it in parallel.*

**Step 1 — Concept Sentence**
> A shiba waits at the door of a house being taken apart around him.

*(Full Steps 2–4 in `04-slate-a-personal.md` § A5.)*

### Why It's Here

Audience Choice pays **$100,000** — level with third place — and it is almost certainly decided by popularity, not by the jury. That is a **different optimization**, and running one film at it costs you very little because this is the cheapest production on either slate.

- **No dialogue** → no language barrier → it travels globally.
- **One fixed camera position** across the whole film → consistency is trivial, and the stillness is the style.
- **A dog** → the single most reliably shareable subject on the internet, deployed for something that isn't cheap.
- **Four minutes** → and people will finish it.

**Producibility:** ★★★★★ — one hallway, one dog, one camera position, ~43 shots. Changes are light angle and set dressing only.
**Risk:** sentimentality. The whole film lives or dies on **restraint**. No score until 3:00. No cutaway to a photograph of the owner. No dissolve. The audience must assemble the fact themselves, and the film must never say it. If it says it, it's a greeting card.

**Score projection:** modest jury scores, **highest Social Media Engagement of anything here**, and a genuine shot at the $100K audience prize.

# B4 · CEPHAS — FIRST DREAM
### *The IP play. Third priority — only if the calendar holds.*

**Step 1 — Concept Sentence**
> A boy who dreams the Titans' war must decide whether waking up is a betrayal.

*(Full Steps 2–4 in `04-slate-a-personal.md` § A3.)*

### Why It's Here

You are a **published epic-fantasy novelist adapting your own book.** *Stone of Matter*, Legend of Litiguh #1, 2019. That is a Social Engagement asset no other entrant has, and it is the one entry that turns the contest into a business move for DarkHearts rather than a lottery ticket — a proof-of-concept for a world you already own outright.

### Why It's Third

1. **Most expensive shot list on either slate** by a wide margin: Titans, scale, a tropical island, water, and crowd. That is four hard problems in one film, at a ~10% keep rate, in 23 days.
2. **Adaptation risk is structural.** Chapter-one adaptations play as trailers. Catmull's test is *"did this person change?"* and Ammon's change in the source material happens across a novel. Beat 5 must land as a genuine ending — a boy who has *already* crossed the line — or the film reads as marketing.
3. It competes for the same slot as SILT: both are your "serious" entry, and SILT is the stronger film.

**If you make it:** cut Beat 2 (the dream) down and give the runtime to Beat 5. The film has to be about the moment *after* he decides, not the spectacle that prompted it.

**Producibility:** ★★☆☆☆
**Score projection:** high creativity ceiling, high Social Engagement, real execution risk.

## The Recommended Portfolio

Entries are unlimited. The correct play is **not one film aimed at every prize** — it is a small slate aimed at different prizes.

| Priority | Film | Target | Effort |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **SILT** | 1st / 2nd / 3rd — the jury prize | ~65% of capacity |
| **2** | **THE DOG AT THE DOOR** | **Audience Choice ($100K)** | ~20% |
| 3 | ROLL | Honorable Mention + technical credibility | ~15%, only if 1 & 2 are locked by Aug 24 |
| 4 | CEPHAS | Next cycle. Build it properly, not in 23 days. | — |

**Why this shape.** SILT and DOG do not compete: one is a jury film about landscape and physics, the other is a wordless interior about waiting. They share a studio signature (`stone remembers, water carries, sound gets it out` — DOG's version is silence) but they will never be compared to each other.

> **Correction to an earlier version of this recommendation.** Entries are unlimited, but **prizes are not** — one prize per participant, with the higher placement winning and the other cascading. Running two films therefore does **not** double your expected payout. What it does is raise the probability of landing *anywhere* in the fourteen, which is the actual goal given that ten of the fourteen slots are $10,000 Honorable Mentions.
>
> That still justifies the slate — DOG costs roughly 20% of your capacity and buys a second, uncorrelated shot at the board — **provided** it never eats into SILT's quality. If look dev slips past Aug 16, cut DOG without hesitation.
>
> The open question is whether **Audience Choice** falls inside or outside that cap. Outside, and the slate is straightforwardly excellent. Inside, and DOG is insurance rather than upside. Verify it (`01-contest-brief.md` §6, question 6) before you commit capacity on Aug 22.

**Do not attempt all four.** Twenty-three days, ~1.6% keep rates, and a solo operator. Two finished films beat four unfinished ones, and an unfinished film scores zero.
