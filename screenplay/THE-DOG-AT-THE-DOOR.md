# THE DOG AT THE DOOR

**A DarkHearts Ltd. Short Film**
Written & Directed by B. L. Barkey
Draft 1 · 2026-08-09 · 4 min 12 sec · 43 shots

> **No dialogue.** There is not one intelligible word in this film. Voices occur — muffled, in other rooms, never resolving into language.

> **The camera is eighteen inches off the floor and it never moves.** Four locked positions, one 35mm lens, no pans, no pushes, no drift. Every human in this film is cropped at the chest or lower — until one of them kneels.

---

```
                        THE DOG AT THE DOOR

                        written & directed by
                           B. L. Barkey


     FADE IN:


     INT. HALLWAY -- MORNING

     A narrow hallway in a small, old, ordinary house. Oak
     floorboards worn pale in a patch in front of the door.
     Cream walls. Scuffed baseboards.

     At the end of it: a front door with two tall panes of glass
     in the upper half. Morning comes through them and lays two
     hard bars of light down the boards, straight at us.

     Lying in the middle of the hallway, square in the light,
     facing the door, his back to us --

     A SHIBA INU.

     Red and cream. Chin flat on the boards. Ears back. Eyes
     open.

     He is the only color in the house.

     Along the right-hand wall, cardboard boxes. Some taped shut.
     Some still open.

     A refrigerator hums somewhere. A clock. A car goes by
     outside and doesn't stop.

     Nothing else happens.

     TITLE:              THE DOG AT THE DOOR

     Held. Eight seconds. Long enough to understand that we are
     not going to be shown anything we do not look for.


     ON THE COAT HOOK -- SAME

     Brass, at adult shoulder height.

     A leather leash, worn soft at the loop.

     Behind it, a man's canvas work jacket, hanging by the collar
     the way a jacket hangs when it has been taken off in the
     same motion every day for years.

     At the bottom of the frame, two small triangular ears.


     BACK TO THE HALLWAY

     A pair of LEGS crosses the frame. Jeans. Work boots. They
     carry a flattened cardboard box.

     They step around the dog.

     Not over. Around -- the small automatic courtesy you extend
     to furniture.

     He does not move.


     THROUGH THE LIVING ROOM DOOR

     A carpeted room. Faded floral wallpaper.

     A brown corduroy RECLINER faces away from us toward a
     window we cannot see.

     In the seat cushion there is a depression. Not a wrinkle --
     a shape. A weight was in that chair for a long time.

     A green plaid blanket is folded over the right arm.

     A ceramic water bowl sits on the carpet beside it.

     Boxes are stacked behind the chair.

     Nobody is in it.


     HALLWAY -- LATER

     The bars of light have moved four inches.

     One box is gone.

     The dog has not moved.


     THE THRESHOLD

     Close on the bottom of the front door. A seam of daylight
     underneath it.

     A SHADOW crosses the seam, left to right. Someone on the
     sidewalk. Footsteps, going away.

     The dog's front paws slide forward. One inch.

     The footsteps are gone.


     BACK TO THE HALLWAY

     His ears go down.


     HALLWAY -- LATE AFTERNOON

     The light bars have climbed off the floor and onto the left
     wall, long and orange.

     Two more boxes.

     He is exactly where he was.

     From somewhere in the house, a WOMAN'S VOICE. Muffled
     through a wall. We do not get the words. A drawer closes.


                                                        CUT TO:


     INT. HALLWAY -- AFTERNOON

     The hall runner is gone. Bare boards.

     He is lying on the boards now. Same spot.


     THROUGH THE LIVING ROOM DOOR

     Half the boxes are gone.

     On the wallpaper above the chair there is a rectangle of
     paper that is cleaner than the paper around it.

     Something hung there for a very long time and now it
     doesn't.

     The recliner is still there.


     HALLWAY -- NIGHT

     The door glass is a grey rectangle with nothing behind it.

     The dog is a silhouette. One bare bulb in a back room lights
     him from behind, wrongly, and catches in his eyes.

     He has not moved.

     The refrigerator hums.

     That is the only sound in the world.


                                                        CUT TO:


     INT. HALLWAY -- MORNING (DAY TWO)

     Hard bars, back on the floor, pointing at us.

     Two sets of MOVERS' LEGS carry a couch through the frame,
     turning it to clear the walls.


     THE THRESHOLD

     A hand comes down and puts a wedge under the door.

     The door swings wide and stays.

     Flat white daylight floods the hall.

     He squints.

     He stays.


     BACK TO THE HALLWAY

     Through the open door: a lawn. A street. A moving truck with
     its ramp down.

     The entire outside world, standing open, unguarded, four
     feet from a dog who is not tied to anything.

     He faces it.

     He does not go.


     THROUGH THE LIVING ROOM DOOR

     The bookshelf is gone.

     Four pale rectangles on the wallpaper now.

     The recliner sits alone in the middle of the floor with the
     blanket still folded over its arm.


     BACK TO THE HALLWAY

     A WOMAN'S LEGS -- jeans, canvas sneakers -- stop beside him.

     A hand comes down into the frame with the ceramic water
     bowl. Sets it by his nose. Withdraws.

     She does not touch him.

     He does not look at her.

     The legs go away.


     ON THE COAT HOOK

     The jacket is gone.

     The leash is still there.


     HALLWAY -- EVENING (DAY TWO)

     The door is shut again. The hallway is completely bare --
     no boxes, no runner, nothing along either wall.

     The bowl is beside him. Full. Untouched.


     HALLWAY -- CONTINUOUS

     The refrigerator STOPS.

     Not fades. Stops.

     His ears come up.

     For one second the house is a place where something might be
     about to happen.

     His ears go back down.

     The silence that is left is enormous.


     THE THRESHOLD

     Rain on the other side of the glass, running down it.

     Grey light. His paws.


     BACK TO THE HALLWAY

     Grey rain-light on the empty boards.

     Nothing in the hallway but a dog and a bowl.


     THROUGH THE LIVING ROOM DOOR

     Nothing left but the recliner.

     Pale rectangles everywhere on the wallpaper, in the shapes
     of a whole life, none of them explained.

     The blanket, still folded over the arm.


                                                        CUT TO:


     INT. HALLWAY -- MORNING (DAY THREE)

     Clean hard light. Two bars on the boards, pointing at us.

     It is the first shot of the film again, exactly -- same
     frame, same hour, same light --

     with everything in the world removed from it except the dog.

     Held.

     He has not moved.


     THROUGH THE LIVING ROOM DOOR

     Two sets of MOVERS' LEGS come in and take up the recliner,
     one at each end.

     The blanket slides off the arm and drops onto the carpet.

     Nobody picks it up.


     BACK TO THE HALLWAY

     The chair comes down the hallway toward us, carried high,
     filling the frame.

     It passes over him and out the front door.

     And his HEAD TURNS to follow it.

     The first time in four minutes that he has turned his head.


     THE THRESHOLD

     The chair goes down the front steps and out of frame.

     The door stands open on a grey afternoon.


     THROUGH THE LIVING ROOM DOOR

     The room is empty.

     In the middle of the carpet: a rectangle where the pile is
     clean and unfaded, with four small round dents in it.

     The blanket on the floor beside it.


     BACK TO THE HALLWAY

     THE DOG STANDS UP.

     His nails come down on the floorboards. It is the first time
     in the film that we have heard him move at all.

     He does not go to the door.

     He breathes out, once, long.


     HALLWAY -- CONTINUOUS

     He walks out of the frame to the left, into the living room
     doorway.

     His nails go away across the boards.

     And for the first time in this film, the hallway is empty.

     The door is open. The light is on the floor. There is
     nobody in it.


     INT. LIVING ROOM -- CONTINUOUS

     He comes in.

     He crosses the bare carpet to the rectangle.

     He turns around once, the way a dog does.

     And he lies down inside the shape of the chair.

     Chin flat. Ears back. Eyes open.

     Facing nothing.

     Silence. No music. Nothing helps.


     LIVING ROOM -- CONTINUOUS

     Held.

     The blanket is a foot from his nose.

     He does not touch it.


                                                        CUT TO:


     INT. LIVING ROOM -- LATER

     He is still in the rectangle.

     Behind him, in the doorway, a pair of LEGS stops.


     INT. HALLWAY -- CONTINUOUS

     The front door, standing open on a flat grey afternoon.

     A WOMAN comes in and stops just inside it.

     Forty. Dark hair pulled back. Tired.

     She does not call him.

     She does not say his name, or click her tongue, or slap her
     thigh, or crouch and open her arms.

     She looks left, into the living room, for a long moment.

     Under everything, so far down that it might be the
     refrigerator coming back on, a single low tone begins.

     It is not the refrigerator. The refrigerator is gone.


     ON THE COAT HOOK

     Her hand comes into the frame and lifts the leash off it.

     A small metal click.

     The hook is empty.


     THE THRESHOLD

     She crouches down in the open doorway.

     Down onto the sill, all the way down, elbows on her knees,
     the leash held in both hands.

     And because she is on the floor now, we can see her face.

     It is the only face in this film.

     She is not smiling. She is not crying. She is not doing
     anything at all.

     She waits.


     INT. LIVING ROOM

     The dog, in the rectangle, looking at her.

     Not moving.


     THE THRESHOLD

     She waits.

     She does not move, does not speak, does not reach.

     She simply stays down there, at his height, for as long as
     it takes.


     INT. HALLWAY

     He comes out of the living room and crosses the hallway to
     the door.

     Nails on floorboards. The second time. The last time.


     THE THRESHOLD

     He reaches her.

     She clips the leash onto his collar.

     And she stands up --

     and her face goes out of the frame, because the camera is
     eighteen inches off the floor and it does not move for
     anybody.

     Only her legs are left. And the dog. And the leash between
     them.


     INT. HALLWAY

     Through the open door: two legs and a dog go down the front
     steps and out of frame.

     Street sound. Then not.

     The door is left standing open.


     ON THE COAT HOOK

     Empty.

     Bare wall, and a brass hook, and nothing hanging on it.


     INT. LIVING ROOM

     The empty room.

     The clean rectangle in the carpet, with its four dents.

     The blanket on the floor beside it.

     Grey light through a window we never see.

     Nobody.

     The tone stops. Not fading. Stopping.


                                                       CUT TO:


     BLACK.


                                                      THE END
```

## Notes on the Draft

**On the absence of an end card.** There isn't one, and that is a decision rather than an oversight. Anything a card could say here, the film has already said better with a rectangle in a carpet. Hold black for two seconds, then the studio mark and the required watermark, and nothing else. *(See `docs/12-dog-full-build.md` — "What Is Deliberately Absent," item 11, and the one alternate worth shooting.)*

**On the woman never calling him.** This is the load-bearing choice in Beat 4 and it will be the first thing anyone suggests changing. Calling him would ask him to give something up. Kneeling asks him nothing — it just puts a person on the floor at his height and leaves the decision where it belongs. It is also the only way the film's one human face gets into the frame, and the only way it can leave again. **She does not speak, and she does not reach.**

**On what the film never shows.** There is no photograph of the man, no flashback, no shot of an empty bed, no funeral clothes, no urn, no date on anything. The chair is the photograph and the rectangle is the grave. If you are tempted to add one image that confirms it — don't. The confirmation is what the audience is *for*.

**On the dog's performance.** He has one posture and he holds it, unbroken, from the first frame to 2:41. Chin flat, ears back, eyes open, facing the door. He is not sad; he is *waiting*, which is a physical state and not an emotional one, and the difference between those two things is the difference between this film and a commercial for pet insurance. Direct the body. Never direct the feeling.
